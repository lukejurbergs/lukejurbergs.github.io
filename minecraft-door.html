The Work

I painted my bedroom door to look exactly like a Minecraft spruce door. Grid by grid, plank by plank, matching the color values as close as I could. I did it alone, and it took longer than you'd think to get the shading right at that scale.

This might look trivial on a portfolio. It isn't to me. Minecraft built my childhood. During COVID I built entire medieval cities, block by block, spending months on structures nobody would ever see except me. I built redstone systems inside underground bunkers: custom piston doors, airlocks, automated lighting, lockdown systems, armor display swaps, hidden crafting tables. Minecraft electronics. The game taught me that building things in detail, for the satisfaction of the system working, was worth the time it took.

The door is the first thing I see when I walk into my room. It's a reminder of who I'm trying to be: someone who builds things, who solves problems, who sees a system and wants to understand it and then improve it. That's the same person who is now building hovercrafts and robotic vehicles. The door is just where it started.

What I Learned

01
Building in detail for no external reason is still worth it. The medieval cities I built in Minecraft during COVID had no audience and no grade attached. Nobody asked me to build them. That's the purest form of the engineering drive: you build it because you want to see if you can.
02
Constraints in Minecraft trained constraint thinking in real engineering. Redstone works in a grid, on and off signals, no exceptions. Designing complex systems inside those limits is genuinely hard. That kind of thinking carries over.
