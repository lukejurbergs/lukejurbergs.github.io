The Challenge

Science Olympiad's Hovercraft event sounds deceptively simple: build a vehicle that travels down a 185cm track and arrives in a specified time window between 6 and 26 seconds. But the devil is in the constraints. The track is only guaranteed to be smooth, not level. You get two motors maximum. No microchips. No springs. No stored energy sources outside the battery. The hovercraft must actually hover — not slide.

Most teams control their timing with a rheostat — a variable resistor that adjusts motor speed. Set it to the right resistance, and theoretically the hovercraft travels at the right speed for the right amount of time. But on an unlevel track, gravity changes everything. A rheostat approach can never account for a surface that tilts up or down by even a fraction of a degree. Every team using that method is essentially hoping the track is flat. Ours didn't bet on it.

Design Evolution

V1
Fan Blocker

The first approach was straightforward: one fan for lift, one for thrust, with a mechanical blocker that partially obstructed the thrust fan's airflow to slow the craft down. Adjust the blocker position, adjust the speed. Simple in theory — but getting the exact right position was inconsistent from run to run, and any surface variation made the results unpredictable.

Single thrust fan Manual blocker Inconsistent results
V2
Double Custom Centrifugal Fan + Precision Blocker

We designed and built custom centrifugal fans from scratch using RC motors — far more efficient than off-the-shelf propellers for this application. The blocker became a precision-machined mechanism, not a hand-set one. Performance improved dramatically, but the rheostat-based speed control still left us at the mercy of surface levelness.

Custom centrifugal fans RC motors Precision blocker Better consistency
V3
"Mechanical Programming" — The Breakthrough

The insight that changed everything: what if the motor IS the timer? Instead of using a microchip or a separate timing circuit (both banned), we used a cam-based mechanical system where the motor's rotation directly controlled a sequence of mechanical events. The motor didn't just drive — it kept time. The cam profile was the program.

This forced a major concession: with both motors now committed — one to lift/thrust and one to timing — we had to combine lift and thrust under a single fan. That's a harder fluid dynamics problem, but it was the only path forward within the rules. We solved it with a thrust nozzle that redirected airflow.

The Struggles

One Month, Every Night

The V3 breakthrough happened just one month before State competition. That meant 30 days of rebuilding from the ground up while still going to school — staying up late every single night, reworking parts, re-testing on weekends. Secondary concerns like packability for travel and how levelness would affect V3's performance on an unknown track couldn't get the attention they needed. At State, those gaps showed.

The Uncontrollable Environment

The rules only guarantee a smooth track, not a level one. Every conventional speed-control method fails the moment the surface tilts. Our cam-based system was immune to this problem in theory — but getting the cam profile exactly right for any surface angle requires testing on different inclines, which we ran out of time to do fully before State.

Collaboration Under Pressure

My partner and I disagreed on almost every design decision — every day, for months. Over 1,200 messages sent. That tension was uncomfortable and exhausting, but it also meant every decision got challenged from two angles. It was the most difficult collaborative environment I'd been in, and ultimately the most successful one.

"The rules prohibit springs and stored energy sources. Cam timing works. The motor is the clock. That was the whole key — stop fighting the constraints and start using the constraints."

What I Learned

01
Unconventional thinking wins constrained problems. When every team is using rheostats and the surface is never guaranteed level, the winning move isn't to optimize the rheostat — it's to abandon rheostats entirely and rethink control from first principles.
02
Uncontrollable environments demand robust design. Engineering for a guaranteed-level surface and engineering for an unknown surface are completely different problems. The hardest part isn't building the device — it's accounting for what you can't control.
03
Productive disagreement is rare and valuable. 1,200+ messages with my partner, most of them arguments. But when two people who actually care both push back on every decision, the design gets stronger. Comfortable agreement produces mediocre work.
04
Fluid dynamics in practice. Centrifugal vs. axial fan efficiency. Thrust vs. lift airflow separation. Nozzle geometry and velocity. None of it was from a textbook — all of it was from building something that needed to actually work.
