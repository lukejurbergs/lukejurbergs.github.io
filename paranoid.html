The Story

Ariya is a 19-year-old woman living alone. She works constantly, comes home exhausted, and starts noticing things that shouldn't be happening. First, a painting fell from the wall. In the middle of the night, a cup shatters. Her cat, Finn, goes missing. After complaining, her landlord's son visits and calls her dramatic and paranoid. She talks to her friend James, who chalks it up to stress. As reality starts to descend into madness, she installs security cameras for proof, and a camera in the attic goes offline.

She goes up herself. She finds meat with her cat's collar in it. Then, from behind, a man crawls out. He knows her name. When she tries to escape, he pushes her down the ladder. Later, the detective arrives and writes "no foul play" on his report, assuming it was a suicide. "Everyone said she was crazy," he tells his partner. Then a new family with children moves in.

The film isn't just a scary horror movie about a phrogger living in one's house like a parasite. My team also wanted to present a message about trust and social/systemic inequalities against women, with the dismissal of a woman's own account of her experience by everyone around her, over and over, until it's too late. Every character Ariya interacts with calls her delusional: the landlord's son is condescending, and her friend doesn't believe her, boiling it down to stress, and even in her final chance at redemption after death, the detective just calls her mentally ill.

Style and Tone

The film gets increasingly claustrophobic as it goes. Early scenes are wide, lit, and open. By the time Ariya is alone at her dining table with a laptop notification telling her the attic camera is offline, the framing has closed in and the colors are cold blues and harsh reds.

The film leaves the viewer just as unsure about Ariya's reality as herself. You never fully see the Phrogger until the end, only shadows and crumbs of food. This effect was intentionally cultivated by also mixing reality with impossibilities. She dreams of flesh and blood dripping, hallucinates a man over her bed while she's sleeping. It distorts truth until the viewer doesn't know if she is truly just crazy or a victim.

On Directing

Directing Paranoid was one of my first experiences as the leader of a large project, with more than 14 people on the film project. The most valuable leadership skill has really influenced my passion for engineering, but it was working shoulder-to-shoulder with my team. Rather than yell out demands like "we'll fix it in post!" I took roles that let me understand the demands and constraints of different roles. I wasn't just a director, but a co-editor, an actor, and a writer. I helped build the set, covered for the sound crew, and the AD.

That decision changed how I understood leadership. When I was playing Arthur in the dining room scene, I understood what Kavya was working against on the other side of the table. When I was in the edit late at night, I understood what it felt like to be the person staring at a Premiere Pro timeline at 3 am before "upload day." Working across every role didn't make the directing harder. It made it better because I actually understood the problems my team was dealing with. Throughout the film, I was constantly asking my Cinematographer how to improve my vision, and I took so many suggestions from my team that skyrocketed the quality of the film.

What I Learned

01
Real leadership means being in it with your team. The best call I made was choosing to act and edit in addition to directing. Every person on the team knew I understood their job because I was doing it alongside them.
02
Quality work means being open to multiple people's visions. Many of my favorite scenes were not as I envisioned; instead, listening to the ideas of my team and being open to changing my vision led to the best product.
03
Where the tension lives isn't where you think. Every instinct says the scariest scene is the attic. But the scenes in the dining room and the kitchen, where Ariya is dismissed, are actually the most disturbing to watch. The film taught me that horror is in the before, not the during.
