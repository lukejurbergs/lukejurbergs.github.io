The Story

Ariya is a 19-year-old living alone. She works constantly, comes home exhausted, and starts noticing things that shouldn't be happening. A painting falls from the wall. A cup shatters in the kitchen at night. Her cat goes missing. She tells her landlord's son, who calls her dramatic and paranoid. She tells her friend James, who chalks it up to stress. She installs security cameras, and then the one in the attic goes offline.

She goes up herself. There's been someone living in her attic for months. He knows her name. When she tries to escape, he pushes her down the ladder. The detective who arrives writes "no foul play" on his report. "Everyone said she was crazy," he tells his partner. A new family moves in.

The film isn't really about the phrogger. It's about what happens when a woman's own account of her experience is dismissed by everyone around her, over and over, until it's too late. Every character who doesn't believe Ariya is played with a specific flavor of dismissal: the landlord's son is condescending, her friend is well-meaning but useless, the detective doesn't care. The film wanted the audience to feel how exhausting that is before the ending made it final.

Style and Tone

The film gets increasingly claustrophobic as it goes. Early scenes are wide, lit, open. By the time Ariya is alone at her dining table with a laptop notification telling her the attic camera is offline, the framing has closed in. The color desaturates. The camera stops giving her room to breathe in the frame.

The moments of danger aren't drawn out. The death is sudden and quick, exactly how those things actually happen, which was an intentional choice. The horror isn't in the violence. It's in the twenty minutes before it where everyone had a chance to believe her and didn't.

On Directing

Directing Paranoid was the first time I was genuinely responsible for something other people were counting on. And I made a specific choice going in: I wasn't going to be a director who hands out assignments and steps back. I acted in it. I helped with set design. I co-edited it. By the time we were done I had been in almost every part of the process.

That decision changed how I understood leadership. When I was playing Arthur in the dining room scene, I understood what Kavya was working against on the other side of the table. When I was in the edit late at night, I understood what it felt like to be the person staring at a timeline the night before a deadline. Working across every role didn't make the directing harder. It made it better, because I actually understood the problems my team was dealing with.

What I Learned

01
Real leadership means being in it with your team. The best call I made was choosing to act and edit in addition to directing. Every person on the team knew I understood their job because I was doing it alongside them. That's not a management technique. It's just the right way to work.
02
The story has to be simple enough to be intentional. Paranoid has a clear message and a straightforward plot, which sounds like a limitation. It's actually what gave us room to be specific about every shot, every costume choice, every color decision. Complex stories leave no room for craft. Simple ones demand it.
03
Where the tension lives isn't where you think. Every instinct says the scariest scene is the attic. But the scenes in the dining room and the kitchen, where Ariya is dismissed, are actually the most disturbing to watch. The film taught me that horror is in the before, not the during.
