On Leadership

Paranoid was the first time I was fully responsible for something other people were counting on. And I made a decision early on: I wasn't going to be a director who hands out roles and walks away. I acted in it. I co-edited it. I helped build the sets.

I knew what it felt like to be the editor staring at a timeline at 3 a.m. the night before upload day — because I was there. I knew how hard it was to get your lines right under pressure — because I was in the shots. When something frustrated a teammate, I understood why, because I'd been doing the same thing.

"The best work doesn't come from leaders who give orders. It comes from someone who knows when to lead and when to get in the trenches — and isn't afraid to do both at the same time."

That's the model I want to bring to engineering. A mechanical engineering team doesn't need a leader who tells the electrical engineer what to do. It needs someone who can sit down with them, understand their constraints, and help find a path neither of you would have found alone. That's what directing Paranoid taught me leadership actually is.

What I Learned

01
Real leadership is participatory. The director who also acts knows what their cast is feeling. The engineer who also codes knows what the software team is up against. Titles don't earn respect — shared experience does.
02
Every role has invisible difficulty. Before I edited, I thought editing was just cutting clips. After 3am timelines, I understood it's creative decision-making under exhaustion. Working every role gave me genuine respect for everyone's contribution.
