The Work

The design is a tortoise with a shell composed of a mystical ecosystem of mushrooms and ferns growing across its back.

The project was nearly a disaster. When the deadline arrived, I had barely touched the linoleum. To finish it, I gave up my lunch period for 10 days straight and carved during the break, every day, until it was done. In the end, I had a beautiful final product and finished on time, even if fitting in eating was a struggle.

Art 1 grading for the entire year was harsh, but there were many chances. First attempt: 60. Work some more. Second attempt: 75. Third: 90. Fourth: 95. That structure forced me to improve and put in a ton of effort past completion, allowing me to learn and grow. I remember a charcoal drawing where I particularly struggled making a high quality piece of art, I had to resubmit like 10 times and come into tons of lunch office hours.

The Charcoal Resubmissions

A separate assignment, meant to be short and quick, from the same class, which reinforced the message about how "finished" is not truly finished. It was a still life in charcoal, and I had to make correction after correction, starting with a terrible grade and reworking it to a better one after many lunch office hours. Four submissions are shown here to represent the progression from the first attempt to the one that finally got an okay grade.

submission 1 — dark is defined by where details are, not brightness
submission 2 — better but there is harsh outlines like a sketch, and the 3D structure is off
submission 3 — shading improving, 3D structure improved
submission 4 — finally got it

What I Learned

01
"I'm Finished!" is not truly finished. Submitting my hard work and receiving low grade after low grade and having to keep working was brutal, but really it was better than submitting once and getting a 100. By the fourth pass, the carving's lighting and detail had improved massively.
02
Every carved line is permanent. Unlike drawing, where you can erase, linoleum is additive subtraction. You remove material and it stays removed. Every cut has to be intentional because you can't undo it, which makes you slow down and think before you cut. I remember messing up one fern (now ultra-bright), and having to embrace imperfection and accept it. This lesson has applied to so many other aspects of my life, and I even created a short film partially about embracing imperfection: Perfectly Alone.
