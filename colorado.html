The Problem

A hillside property in Colorado with a serious erosion problem and no safe way to walk between the backyard and the sideyard. Without intervention, you had to scamper along a steep, rock-filled drop-off like a mountain goat. The hillside was actively washing soil downhill. There was no trail, no stairs, and no budget to speak of — just a drill, a shovel, a hammer, a pickaxe, a wheelbarrow, and less than five dollars of new materials per day of work.

"I'd noticed deer using the hillside as a passage between two forested areas. After I built the trail, I realized I hadn't just solved a human problem — I'd also improved a wildlife corridor."

What Was Built

01
Erosion Barriers + Stairs

Railroad timbers secured with lag screws and stakes along the hillside, terracing the slope into manageable steps. This stopped the soil washout and created a safe trail from backyard to sideyard — accounting for tree root spacing so the trees weren't damaged in the process. The trail is now used by wildlife, including deer moving between forest areas.

02
Rock Retaining Wall + Hammock Spot

Rocks scattered around the yard (previously safety hazards — loose rocks on a hillside = injury risk) were gathered and stacked into a retaining wall along the cliffside trail. The wall created a flat, shaded, accessible spot — which I landscaped specifically to support a hammock setup. What was hazardous became a destination.

03
Scrap-Only Additions

A firewood holder built from storm-fallen branches, unused screws, and rope — keeping wood off the ground to prevent rot. A compost bin to promote sustainable practices. Dozens of boards of scrap wood stored under the deck (rather than scattered as a nail hazard). All from materials already on the property, cost: $0.

04
Demolition: Unsafe Greenhouse

An old, unpermitted greenhouse on the property had deteriorated to the point of being a structural safety hazard. Demolished it cleanly, cleared the site. Sometimes engineering is knowing what needs to go.

What I Learned

01
Engineering with almost nothing. Every professional project has a budget and a supply chain. This one didn't. Solving real structural problems with storm branches, salvaged rope, and a five-dollar daily limit forces creative problem-solving that classroom projects don't.
02
Environmental responsibility is part of the design. Tree root spacing, soil preservation, wildlife corridors — these weren't add-ons. They were constraints that shaped every decision. Good physical design accounts for the ecosystem it touches.
03
This is where I fell in love with tools. The first summer in Colorado, working with a drill and a pickaxe on real problems, was where I realized I wanted to spend my life building things. Not as a memory — as a direction.
