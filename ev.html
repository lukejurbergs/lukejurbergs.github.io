Starting at Zero

I started this project knowing absolutely nothing about electronics. I didn't know what an encoder was. I'd never written a line of C++ that controlled physical hardware. I'd never soldered a circuit under pressure. Everything I know about electronics — I learned building this car.

The goal: a vehicle that travels a specified distance (7–10 meters) in a specified time (10–20 seconds), while navigating 1 meter to the left through an offset pair of cans. The closer you squeeze through the cans, the more bonus points you earn. Simple enough to describe. Hellishly difficult to execute with the required precision.

"I had the belief that 90-degree steering would be the simplest thing ever. It turned out to be the most difficult path — because although it looks simple, reaching PRECISION with that design was impossibly hard."

Design Evolution

V1
Right Angle Travel

The first instinct: drive straight, turn 90°, drive straight again. Two legs. One turn. It seems like the simplest possible design. It wasn't. Getting a precise 90° turn with enough repeatability to score at competition turned out to be nearly impossible. Any wheel slip, any fabrication imprecision, any encoder error, and the angle drifts. The "simple" design demanded perfection from every component.

Two-segment path 90° steering High sensitivity to error
V2
Bi-Stable Right Angle Travel

A more mechanically locked version of the 90° turn — using a bi-stable mechanism so the turn was physically constrained into position rather than relying purely on encoder feedback. Better repeatability, but still subject to wheel slipping and fabrication imprecision causing cumulative angle error.

Bi-stable mechanism Improved repeatability Still encoder-sensitive
V3
Upgrading Off-the-Shelf Kit with Encoders

Pivoted to building on an existing kit (UnPhayzed) and adding our own angle measurement system. This let me learn encoder integration and C++ motor control without building the chassis from scratch simultaneously. Good for learning — but the kit's geometry limited what I could achieve with arc-based travel.

UnPhayzed kit base Encoder integration C++ control loop
V4
Custom Ultra-Thin Arc Car — The Final Design

Built from scratch. The key innovations: the encoder was repositioned to a vertical orientation using belt drives — eliminating backlash while achieving a sub-60mm vehicle width that could squeeze through the tightest can gaps. A custom-built gearbox gave the motor output the right torque and speed profile. Arc-based travel replaced right-angle turns entirely.

The insight driving V4 was the simplicity paradox: differential steering looks complex but IS simple, because it allows PID loops and active correction that make the path naturally self-correcting. 90° turns look simple but are impossible to make precise.

The Struggles

The Source of Failure Was Never Obvious

With this many variables — math calculations, mechanical slipping, encoder drift, voltage instability, code bugs — pinpointing why the car ended up in the wrong place was a debugging nightmare. Every run that missed could have failed for a dozen different reasons. Isolating variables became its own engineering challenge.

Absolute Encoders Drifting Unpredictably

Encoders that should read a consistent angle started drifting between runs with no obvious cause. Voltage instability, mechanical vibration, temperature — all plausible culprits. The passive steering mechanism meant there was no active correction to catch drift mid-run.

No Space to Test at Home

You need 10 meters of clear, smooth floor to test an EV properly. We didn't have that. Most testing happened on a nearby elementary school's basketball court — many evenings from 6–10pm, brushing dust and pebbles off the concrete before every run. Not glamorous, but it worked.

What I Learned

01
All of my electronics knowledge. Came in knowing nothing. Left understanding motor drivers, encoder integration, voltage regulation, C++ PWM control, and circuit debugging at a practical level. Every skill I now have in electronics came from this project.
02
What "simple" actually means. Simple is not what looks simple. Simple is what is actually easy to make precise, correct, and robust. 90° turns look simple — they aren't. Differential steering with PID looks complex — it's actually the easiest path to a working car.
03
How to fail productively. Fried circuit boards. Solder burns. Entire design approaches abandoned after weeks of work. The project was defined by failure — and each failure narrowed the solution space. Learning what doesn't work is still learning.
04
Tons of C++. Arrays, control loops, PID implementation, serial communication with sensors. This project was my real C++ education — not a class, but a deadline with real stakes.
