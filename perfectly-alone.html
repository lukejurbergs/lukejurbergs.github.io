The Film

Perfectly Alone is about what happens when you decide the most important thing is fitting in. Getting into LASA, I spent the summer before 9th grade hearing from people that everyone there was trying to be perfect, and that I might not fit in. So I decided I would be perfect. Late nights studying. Grade obsession. Piano practice not because I loved it but because getting it right felt like a way to prove something.

The film follows that arc. Staying up until midnight studying. Entering piano recitals not with excitement but with dread about making mistakes. And then a friend who needed help with math. He would ask for my answers, but when I asked to hang out, he had plans. It clicked: my perfection had made me useful, not loved. I was, as the film puts it, "the answer key in the teacher's drawer." Inanimate. A tool.

The turning point is a piano recital where I made mistake after mistake. And I smiled through all of it. It was my worst performance technically and my best one actually, because I felt connected to the audience, to my piano teacher, to myself, in a way I never had when everything went right. The film ends there, with the argument that imperfection is where real connection lives.

"I couldn't have fit in any better." The phrase repeats every time I betray myself in the film, until the irony becomes impossible to miss.

Craft Choices

The phrase "I couldn't have fit in better" repeats throughout the film, always at moments where fitting in actually means betraying yourself. It starts feeling like an achievement and ends feeling like an indictment. The repetition was the whole device.

The emotional contrast was deliberate too. Early in the film, when I'm being "perfect," I'm almost never smiling unless someone else is watching. Late in the film, when things go wrong at the recital, I'm grinning the whole way through. The camera catches that difference without explaining it.

The language in the voiceover uses dehumanizing words on purpose at certain points. "The answer key in the teacher's drawer." Not a person. An object that exists to serve a function. That phrasing was intentional. That's what perfectionism does to relationships when it's the thing people value in you.

What I Learned

01
Personal films are harder to make than fictional ones. Paranoid is fiction. Perfectly Alone is me, on camera, talking about how I failed at being human for a few years. That takes a different kind of commitment. It also produced a more honest film.
02
Repeating a phrase changes its meaning. "I couldn't have fit in better" starts as pride. By the third time it appears, it's irony. By the end it's self-criticism. The repetition does work that explanation can't.
03
The best grades came after I stopped caring about them. That's the actual ending of the story. Letting go of perfectionism didn't tank my grades. They got better, because I was learning for real reasons instead of to avoid failure. That part surprised me when it happened, and it's what made the film worth making.
