The Story

The diorama is based on one of the most creative escapes in Cold War history. In 1989, two brothers, Thomas and Ingo Bethke, reunited after years on opposite sides of the Berlin Wall by flying a homemade ultralight aircraft from West Germany into East Germany and then out again. The escape was a miracle of planning and nerve. It felt like exactly the right story to build physically, where you could feel the division between the two worlds just by standing in front of it.

The whole point of the design was contrast. The west side is alive with color, painted murals, green grass, and a bright blue and yellow building. The east side is gray, covered in litter, windows near the wall bricked up so nobody inside could see what freedom looked like. In the middle, between those two worlds, is everything the East German state put between people and escape: the watchtower, the patrol path, the anti-vehicle trenches, the barbed wire, the death strip.

The Death Strip

The section between the two walls took the most research and the most time. Every element in the death strip was real and had to be right. The anti-vehicle trenches were carved foam painted brown with real soil modge-podged on top. The barbed wire was floral wire coiled around a pencil then stretched out. The watchtower is clay and PVC pipe, painted, sitting on a carved foam base. The anti-vehicle hedgehogs were popsicle sticks cut and spray painted. The sand strip, which guards could rake to detect footprints, was made by laying down string, taping over it, painting the sand color, then pulling the tape to leave texture lines before adding real sand with modge-podge.

The Aircraft

Suspended above the whole diorama on a dowel is a model of the Microlight Fox II, the actual aircraft the Bethke brothers used. The frame is made from dowel pieces and wooden skewers. The nose cone came from an old cold brew can. The windshield is cut from a plastic binder sheet. Wings are cardboard. The whole thing is camouflage spray painted in dark green, light green, and black, with metal parts hand painted gray. The wheels are a mix of custom 3D printed parts and LEGO motorcycle wheels. Red Soviet Union stars, printed and modge-podged on the wings and tail, are a nod to the irony of the whole escape.

Making It Work

The foam for the whole base came from scrap left over from an acoustic engineering project my dad had done. The problem was it came in one thick sheet, so I had to use a jigsaw to cut it down into the right shapes and layers. From there it was weeks of carving, painting, texturing, and adding detail. Stop signs, pieces of litter, asphalt texture on the east side streets. Every square inch had something going on.

The hardest part was keeping track of what was actually historically accurate. The death strip layout, the specific wall shapes on each side (smooth concrete pipe on top, brick pattern below), the soldier patrol path. Getting those details right was part of what made it a competition piece and not just a model.

What I Learned

01
Using what you have. The foam was scrap. The nose cone was a coffee can. The wheels were partially LEGO. Nothing was bought for a purpose it wasn't also serving somewhere else. That constraint made the construction more creative, not less.
02
Physical scale changes how you understand history. You can read about the Berlin Wall in a paragraph. Building it, even at this scale, changes how you think about it. Physically laying out where each element went, how close the death strip was to the wall, what you could and couldn't see from either side... the diorama made me understand it differently than any textbook did.
03
Contrast as a design tool. The whole thing only works because the two sides are so different from each other. Color, texture, the types of details added. Designing that contrast intentionally and making sure it held up from every viewing angle was a real compositional challenge.
