The Story

The diorama is based on one of the creative escapes across the Berlin Wall, which I found particularly influential because it displays unwavering familial love and the skill of working with little. In 1989, two brothers, Holger and Ingo Bethke, who had already escaped from East Berlin, flew a homemade ultralight aircraft from West Germany into East Germany and then out again, picking up their brother Egbert. I loved how, despite having already escaped, they decided to still save their brother. Buying the ultralight aircraft also required selling all of their possessions, which I find a noble example of radical resourcefulness and determination.

One major goal of the design was to create contrast between the cities to display both the true and metaphorical social divide that the wall created. The west side is alive with color, painted murals, green grass, and a bright blue and yellow building. The east side is gray, covered in litter, windows near the wall bricked up so nobody inside could see what freedom looked like. In the middle, between those two vastly different worlds, is all the evil defenses put between people and escape: the watchtower, the patrol path, the anti-vehicle trenches, the barbed wire, the death strip, and the control strips.

Building the Base

The foam for the whole base came from scrap left over from an acoustic engineering project my dad had done. The problem was that it came in one thick sheet, so I had to use a jigsaw to cut it down into the right shapes and layers, which was especially annoying because it created tons of dust. Before I started building, I marked out every zone from the east wall, patrol path, dirt strip, control strips, sidewalk, park, etc. using masking tape on the foam so I could get the scale right.

The Death Strip

The section between the two walls took the most research and the most time. Every element in the death strip had specific details and purposes I had to figure out, from raked sand paths to watchtowers. The anti-vehicle trenches were carved foam painted brown with real soil modge-podged on top. The barbed wire was floral wire coiled around a pencil, then stretched out. The watchtower is hand-sculpted clay atop a piece of PVC pipe, painted, and sitting on a carved foam base. The anti-vehicle hedgehogs were popsicle sticks cut and spray painted. The control sand strip, which guards could rake to detect footprints, was made by laying down string, taping over it to keep the string in place, painting the sand color, then adding real sand with Mod Podge. The streetlamps are metal wire with a dab of hot glue on the end, then painted black.

The Aircraft

Suspended above the whole diorama on a dowel is a model of the Microlight Fox II, the actual aircraft the Bethke brothers used. The frame is made from dowel pieces and wooden skewers, painted in a metallic gray. The nose cone came from an old cold brew can that I cut and superglued into a cone. The windshield is cut from a plastic binder sheet. Wings are cardboard. The whole thing is camouflage spray-painted in dark green, light green, and black with red star stickers that match the Soviet Union's aircraft to create hesitation in anyone trying to shoot the Bethke brothers down. The wheels are a mix of custom 3D printed parts and LEGO motorcycle wheels.

Making It Work

From there, it was days of carving, painting, texturing, and adding detail. Stop signs, pieces of litter, and asphalt texture on the east side streets.

The hardest part was keeping track of what was actually historically accurate. The death strip layout, the specific wall shapes on each side (smooth concrete pipe on top, brick pattern below), the soldier patrol path. Getting those details right was part of what made it a competition piece and not just a model.

Another struggle was that since many of the materials, including the foam, were scrap, I didn't know about their properties. This foam reacted with superglue and melted from hot glue, making it terrible to attach pieces!

What I Learned

01
Using what you have. The foam was a scrap piece, the plane's nose cone was a coldbrew can, the wheels were half LEGO, and the windshield was an old binder sheet. Nothing was bought for one singular purpose without reusing the material in different ways. That same technique reminded me of the Bethke Brothers: how they sold everything they had to buy the two aircraft.
02
Showing not telling. Many art projects in my past up to this point hadn't had such a deep message, and I never had to show such a complex story in one still. But this diorama was different, I had dozens of differences between the East & West to show and zero words, so I learned how to show without telling. Little details like extra infrastructure, bright paint, murals versus bland sidewalks, no murals, a rougher street, and litter can tell tons of aspects about East and West Berlin.
